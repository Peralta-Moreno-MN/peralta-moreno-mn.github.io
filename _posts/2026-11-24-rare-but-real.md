Rare diseases are not rare for the people who live with them. They are part of every single day,
for patients and for their families. They are just as real as you.
<!--more-->

## Rare, one by one. Common, all together

In Europe, a disease is called *rare* when it affects fewer than 1 in 2,000 people. One by one,
each of them touches very few lives. But there are more than 6,000 different rare diseases, and
when you add them all up, the numbers change completely: **more than 300 million people
worldwide** live with a rare disease. That is around 4% of the world's population.

Most of them are genetic (about 72%), and most of them start in childhood (about 70%).
So behind these numbers there are a lot of children, and a lot of families learning, often far
too late, the name of something that had been changing their lives for years.

## When "treatment" means managing symptoms

Here comes the hardest part: **only around 5% of rare diseases have an approved treatment**.
For the vast majority, what medicine can offer today is palliative care: relieving the
symptoms, slowing things down where possible, making each day a little easier.

That care matters enormously. But it is not enough. With so few patients for each disease,
research has often been slow, and many of these diseases are still waiting for their turn
in the lab.

## But there is hope

Science is moving faster than ever. Genetic tests that took years now take weeks. Gene
therapies that sounded like science fiction are reaching patients. And something that still
amazes me: we can now **design new proteins from scratch**, proteins that nature never made,
built to do jobs that natural proteins can't. This work, led by David Baker, was recognised with
the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, together with the AI that predicts protein structures
(Demis Hassabis and John Jumper).

Designing molecules to overcome the limits of nature. For diseases that nature left without
an answer, that's a pretty good place to start.

## About this series

*Rare but Real* is a series to give visibility to rare diseases: what they are, the people
living with them, the families and associations pushing research forward, and the science
that gives us reasons to hope. Each story will focus on one disease, starting with
[Friedreich's ataxia]({% post_url 2026-11-24-friedreich-ataxia %}) and
[ADSL deficiency]({% post_url 2026-11-24-adsl-deficiency %}).

Because the more we see, the more we research. And the more we research together, the closer
we get.
