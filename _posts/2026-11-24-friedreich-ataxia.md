It usually starts between the ages of 5 and 15, with something as ordinary as losing your
balance. Friedreich's ataxia is a rare genetic disease that slowly takes away coordination,
strength and, often, the health of the heart.
<!--more-->

## What is it?

Friedreich's ataxia (FA) is an inherited disease of the nervous system. *Ataxia* means loss of
coordination, and that is usually the first sign: unsteady walking and poor balance. Over the
years it can also bring loss of strength and sensation in the arms and legs, muscle stiffness,
difficulties with speech, hearing and vision, scoliosis and, in some people, diabetes.

Many people with FA also develop **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy**, a thickening and weakening of
the heart muscle that can be life-threatening.

It affects about **1 in 40,000–50,000 people**, and it is the most common inherited ataxia.

## A small repeat, a big difference

The cause lies in the *FXN* gene, which encodes **frataxin**, a small protein that works inside
the mitochondria, the energy factories of our cells.

Inside this gene there is a short DNA sequence, **GAA**, that is repeated several times. Most of
us have between 5 and 33 copies. People with FA have from 66 to more than 1,000. This expansion
silences the gene, so cells make much less frataxin. Neurons and heart cells, which need lots of
energy, are the ones that suffer most. The longer the repeat, the earlier the disease usually
starts.

FA is **autosomal recessive**: you need two altered copies of the gene, one from each parent.
Parents are usually healthy carriers who don't know they carry it.

## Treatment: a first step

For a long time, there was nothing to offer beyond managing symptoms. That changed in **2023**,
when **omaveloxolone** (Skyclarys) became the first approved drug for FA, first in the United
States and in **February 2024** in the European Union, for patients aged 16 and older.

Omaveloxolone activates **Nrf2**, a protein that helps cells defend themselves against oxidative
stress, a pathway that is weakened in FA. It does not fix the root cause, but it is the first
treatment shown to slow the progression of the disease. A first step, and a very important one.

## And what's next

Research is now aiming at the root of the problem: bringing frataxin back. **Gene therapies**
that deliver a working copy of *FXN*, especially to the heart, are already being tested in
clinical trials, and other approaches try to reactivate the silenced gene or replace the missing
protein.

Every one of those trials exists because patients and families agreed to take part. That is
also science.
