Fewer than a hundred cases have been described in the whole world. Adenylosuccinate lyase
(ADSL) deficiency is so rare that most doctors will never see a single case. But for the
children who live with it, and for their families, it is everything.
<!--more-->

## What is it?

ADSL deficiency is an inherited metabolic disease that affects the brain. Children with ADSL
deficiency usually have developmental delay, low muscle tone, seizures that are hard to
control and, in some cases, autistic features.

It has three forms, depending on severity:

- **Neonatal form**, the most severe, with signs already before or at birth.
- **Type I** (severe), the most common, with symptoms in the first months of life.
- **Type II** (moderate or mild), where development is usually normal for the first years and
  then slows down.

## One enzyme, one missing step

Our cells build **purines**, the building blocks of DNA, RNA and ATP, the molecule that stores
our energy, step by step, like an assembly line. **Adenylosuccinate lyase**, encoded by the
*ADSL* gene, performs two of those steps.

When the enzyme doesn't work well, the line gets stuck: two intermediates, SAICAR and S-AMP,
pile up and are turned into **SAICAr** and **S-Ado**, which are thought to be toxic for the
brain. These two compounds can be measured in urine and cerebrospinal fluid, and they are the
key to diagnosis. Interestingly, the more SAICAr compared to S-Ado, the more severe the disease
tends to be.

It is **autosomal recessive**: a child needs two altered copies of the gene, one from each
healthy parent.

## No treatment yet

Today there is **no specific treatment** for ADSL deficiency. Care focuses on controlling
seizures and supporting development with therapies, but nothing yet can correct the missing
step.

## Moving forward together

When a disease is this rare, progress happens when families, scientists and society walk
the path together.

That is the story of the **Associació Antian**, created by Queralt Guix and Joan Torres, parents
of Anton and Andreu, two brothers with ADSL deficiency. In 2023 they launched a crowdfunding
campaign through the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona to fund research towards a possible
**gene therapy**, led by Dr. Miguel Chillón and Dr. Assumpció Bosch (Institut de Neurociències
de la UAB and Vall d'Hebron Institut de Recerca).

Every small step counts, and every person who learns about ADSL deficiency brings that path
a little further. Sharing their story is a beautiful way to start.
